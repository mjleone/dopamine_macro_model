Dopamine pathways

A working model of the brain's four main dopamine pathways, from the midbrain and hypothalamus to their targets. Choose a scenario and press play. Click any region to pause and read about it.

About this model

How it works

Each dopamine source fires at a rate set by the scenario: a slow, regular tonic rhythm by default, with bursts and pauses where the scenario calls for them. Dopamine in each target is computed from recent firing, the share of surviving neurons, and any drug effects on synthesis, breakdown, reuptake and release.

Line thickness shows how much dopamine each pathway delivers, from firing, surviving neurons and disease changes. D1 and D2 signaling follow the dopamine level, changed further by drugs acting on terminals and receptors. Region color shows overall dopamine signaling, weighted by each region's mix of D1 and D2 receptors. The basal ganglia loops and the effects readouts are all computed from those signals, so every panel stays consistent with the brain view.

Limitations

Dopamine is reduced to one number per region. Synapse-level details such as transporter kinetics, receptor binding and autoreceptor feedback are left out, except where a scenario describes them.

Firing is treated as the only driver of release. In reality, local circuits in the striatum, especially cholinergic interneurons, can trigger or shape release at the axon terminals independently of firing at the cell bodies.

Distances from the midline are approximate adult human values for a schematic, not atlas measurements. All numbers are relative to baseline and are illustrative, not quantitative predictions. Compensation in early Parkinson's disease is modeled simply, as surviving neurons releasing extra dopamine.

Not included

Minor dopamine systems are left out: the hypothalamospinal projection (linked to restless legs syndrome), the incertohypothalamic system, and dopamine cells in the retina and olfactory bulb.

D2-blocking anti-nausea drugs such as metoclopramide act in the area postrema, a brainstem region outside the blood-brain barrier. It is a receptor site rather than a dopamine pathway, so it is mentioned but not drawn.

Ideas the model simplifies

Reward prediction error is the leading account of phasic dopamine signals, but not the only one. Dopamine neurons also respond to novelty and salience, some respond to aversive events, and incentive salience theory describes dopamine as "wanting" rather than learning.

All atypical antipsychotics are modeled as one drug: 70 percent D2 occupancy plus 5-HT2A blockade, with the extra dopamine release applied only to the putamen and prefrontal cortex. Partial agonists such as aripiprazole are not simulated. The motor loop tolerates D2 blockade up to about 70 to 80 percent occupancy, matching the clinical threshold for motor side effects, while the other targets respond in proportion. D2 supersensitivity in the tardive dyskinesia scenario is limited to the striatum.

The classic schizophrenia hypothesis (high mesolimbic, low mesocortical dopamine) is shown alongside its modern refinement from PET imaging, which places the largest dopamine excess in the associative part of the dorsal striatum.
